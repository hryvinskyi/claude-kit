# Simplified Technical English, as tighten applies it

ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (STE) is a controlled language for technical
documentation. It started in aerospace maintenance, and other fields now use it. The Simplified
Technical English Maintenance Group of ASD keeps the standard. Issue 9 (January 2025) has 53
writing rules in nine sections and a dictionary of about 900 approved words. The standard is free
on request at asd-ste100.org.

This file gives the rules in tighten's words, in the sequence of the standard's sections. It is not
the standard, and it does not contain the dictionary. Tighten writes to the rules. It does not
certify a text, and ASD certifies no tool that does. A deliverable gives the name of its profile. It
never says that it "complies with ASD-STE100".

## Profiles

| Profile | Rules that apply |
|---|---|
| light | the rules with the mark **all** |
| standard | the rules with the marks **all** and **std** |
| strict | every rule, and *The dictionary* below |

The rules with the mark **all** apply in every language. The rule numbers are those of Issue 9.

## The rules by section

### 1. Words

- **strict** Use only the words that the dictionary approves, technical nouns and technical verbs
  (rules 1.1, 1.5, 1.12). A technical noun or a technical verb is a term of the subject field: a
  part, a tool, a unit, a command, a process. In software: "container", "pull request", "to
  deploy", "to commit".
- **strict** Use an approved word only as the part of speech that the dictionary gives, and only
  with the approved meaning (1.2, 1.3).
- **strict** Do not use a technical noun as a verb, or a technical verb as a noun (1.7, 1.13).
- **std** Use one name for one thing (1.11). Use a name that is short and clear (1.9). Do not use
  slang as a name (1.10).
- **strict** Use American spelling, unless the project or the source uses a different spelling
  (1.14). Standard and light keep the spelling of the source.

### 2. Nouns in a row

- **all** Write a maximum of three nouns in a row (2.1).
- **all** When a technical name has more than three words, write it in full one time. Then use a
  shorter name, or connect the closely related words with hyphens (2.2).

### 3. Verbs

- **std** Use only these forms: the infinitive, the command form, the simple present, past and
  future, and the past participle as an adjective (3.2, 3.3).
- **std** Do not make complex verb constructions with auxiliary verbs (3.4). This removes the
  progressive ("is running"), the perfect ("has finished") and a stack of auxiliary verbs ("should
  have been restarted").
- **strict** Use the "-ing" form only as a technical noun or in a technical noun (3.5): "landing
  gear", "load balancing".
- **std** An "-ing" form can be the name of an action: "Deleting a workspace removes its files."
  It cannot attach a second action to a sentence: "after restarting the worker", "…, ensuring that
  the cache is warm". Write a clause with a subject and a verb.
- **std** Use the active voice. In a description, the passive is correct only when the agent is
  unknown (3.6). When the source does not give the agent, keep the passive. Do not invent an agent.
- **std** Use a verb for an action, not a noun (3.7): "adjust", not "make an adjustment".

### 4. Sentences

- **all** Write short and clear sentences (4.1).
- **std** Do not omit words to make a sentence shorter, and do not use contractions (4.2). Keep the
  subject, the verb and the articles.
- **std** Use a vertical list for complex text (4.3).
- **std** Use connecting words between sentences with related topics (4.4): "then", "thus", "but",
  "because", "as a result".
- **std** Put an article, or "this" or "these", before a noun where English needs one (4.5).

### 5. Instructions

- **all** A sentence that gives an instruction has a maximum of 20 words (5.1).
- **all** Write one instruction in each sentence. Two are correct only when the actions occur at
  the same time (5.2).
- **std** Write an instruction as a command (5.3): "Run the script", not "The script must be run".
- **std** When an instruction starts with a condition, put a comma after the condition (5.4): "If
  the queue is empty, restart the worker."
- **std** A note gives information only. It does not give an instruction (5.5).

### 6. Descriptions

- **all** A sentence that describes has a maximum of 25 words.
- **all** A paragraph has a maximum of six sentences and one topic. Its first sentence gives the
  topic.
- **std** Give information gradually: one new fact in each sentence, in the sequence that the
  reader needs. Use the key words of the topic to connect the sentences.

### 7. Warnings

- **std** Start a warning or a caution with a clear command or a condition (7.2). Then give the
  risk or the possible result (7.3).
- **strict** Start it with a word that gives the level of risk (7.1). The usual words are WARNING
  for danger to persons and CAUTION for damage to equipment or data.

### 8. Punctuation and word counts

- **all** Do not use the semicolon. All other standard punctuation is correct (8.1). Tighten's own
  rules also remove em dashes and en dashes.
- **std** Use hyphens to connect closely related words (8.2). Parentheses are correct (8.3).
- **all** Count the words of a sentence as follows (8.4 to 8.7):
  - A colon that introduces a vertical list ends the sentence. Each item of the list has its own
    count.
  - Text in parentheses counts as one word of the sentence.
  - A hyphenated word counts as one word.
  - A quotation and a title count as one word each. A number and an identifier also count as one
    word each.

### 9. Writing practices

- **std** When a word-for-word replacement does not give a correct sentence, use a different
  construction (9.1). "The migration is running" becomes "The migration is in progress".
- **std** Use a one-word verb when it has the same meaning as a phrasal verb: "do", not "carry
  out". Strict does not use phrasal verbs that two approved words make (9.3).
- **std** Keep the wording consistent (9.4). The same instruction has the same words each time.

## The dictionary

The dictionary gives each approved word one part of speech and one meaning, with few exceptions. It
also lists about 1,200 words that are not approved, each with an approved alternative. Technical
nouns and technical verbs are not in the dictionary. The field of the writer supplies them.

Standard uses the principle only: the common word, with one meaning in the text. Strict uses the
entries. The entries below go against everyday English. Each one comes from the published examples
of the standard.

| Word | In STE | Write |
|---|---|---|
| test | a noun only | "Do a test of the system", not "Test the system" |
| close | a verb only | "near" for the adjective: "Do not go near the landing gear" |
| follow | "come after", not "obey" | "Obey the safety instructions" |
| about | "concerned with" only | "approximately 10 minutes" |
| make sure | approved | "Make sure that the switch is off", not "ensure", "verify", "confirm" or "check that" |
| acceptable | not approved | "A value of 2 mm is permitted" |
| begin | not approved | "start" |
| execute, carry out, conduct (a test) | not approved | "Do a test" |
| fall | "move down by gravity" only | "The pressure decreases", not "The pressure falls" |
| oil | a noun only | "The oil is dirty" is correct, "Oil the valve" is not |
| have received | the perfect is not a correct form | "We received the reports" |

Do not say from memory that a word is approved or not approved. A recalled entry is frequently
the opposite of the real one, and "about" and "test" are the usual examples. When the user
supplies the dictionary (the PDF from asd-ste100.org, or its text), find each word there, except
technical terms. Without the dictionary, apply the principle and the table. Then add "vocabulary
not checked against the STE dictionary" to the stat line.

## What strict cannot say

STE has no "I", "he" or "she", and it has no construction for a hypothesis. A sentence that needs
one of these keeps its claim, in the simplest form that can carry the claim. List these sentences in
one line after the stat line. Do not remove the claim, and do not put it in a form that changes it.

## Examples

A procedure. The source:

> Before deploying, you need to ensure that the migration has been run, and then the cache needs to
> be cleared and the workers restarted.

Standard:

> Do these steps before you deploy:
>
> 1. Make sure that the migration is complete.
> 2. Clear the cache.
> 3. Restart the workers.

A description. The source:

> The scheduler, which is responsible for the triggering of jobs, is currently being migrated to the
> new queue, having previously relied on cron.

Standard:

> The scheduler starts the jobs. Before the migration, it used cron. The migration to the new queue
> is in progress.

A warning. The source:

> Note that restarting the worker during a migration should be avoided, as it may result in the loss
> of queued jobs.

Standard:

> Do not restart the worker during a migration. The queue can lose its jobs.

Strict puts "CAUTION:" in front of the command.

A tense that carries a claim. The source:

> We have been seeing timeouts since Monday.

Standard:

> The timeouts started on Monday, and they continue.

## Other languages

STE is a standard for English. In a different language, tighten keeps the rules with the mark
**all**. It applies the rules with the mark **std** through the grammar of that language. In
Ukrainian:

| Rule | Not this | This |
|---|---|---|
| A verb for the action | «здійснити перевірку», «виконати налаштування» | «перевірити», «налаштувати» |
| The active voice | «Файл було видалено системою.» | «Система видалила файл.» |
| A command for a step | «Необхідно виконати перезапуск сервісу.» | «Перезапустіть сервіс.» |
| One instruction in a sentence | «Оновіть конфігурацію, після чого перезапустіть сервіс і перевірте журнал.» | «Оновіть конфігурацію. Потім перезапустіть сервіс. Перевірте журнал.» |
| A maximum of three nouns in a row | «час завершення обробки запиту користувача» | «коли система завершила обробляти запит користувача» |
| A clause with a verb | «Користувач, увійшовши в систему, отримує доступ до звітів.» | «Користувач входить у систему й отримує доступ до звітів.» |
| One term for one thing | «сервіс», then «служба», then «застосунок» | «сервіс» each time |

These rules are only for English and do not apply: the articles, the contractions, the list of verb
forms, American spelling and the dictionary.

## The checker

`scripts/ste_check.py` counts what a reader counts badly: the words of each sentence, the sentences
of each paragraph, the semicolons. In English text it also finds contractions, and it gives the verb
forms and the inflated words that need a second look. SKILL.md shows the command.

The checker reads Markdown. A code span, a link address and a quotation count as one word each. Text
in parentheses counts as one word, and the checker also measures it as a sentence of its own. Code
blocks, front matter, headings and tables are not prose, and the checker skips them.

The checker does not see nouns in a row, the choice of words or the meaning. A `review` line is a
pattern that matched. It can be wrong.

## Sequence of precedence

1. A claim of the source is more important than each rule here. Sometimes only a construction that
   STE does not permit can carry a claim. Then keep the claim, in the simplest such construction.
2. A rule here is more important than a shorter text. Do not remove an article, a subject or a
   connecting word to save a word. The rewrite of a telegraphic source can be longer than the source.
3. In light, the register is more important than the rules with the mark **std**. The voice of a
   person is content.
4. After the humanizer pass, the rules of the profile apply again. Keep the wording of humanizer
   where it obeys them.
